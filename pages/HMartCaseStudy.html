H Mart: an FMCG distributor in Power BI

Two years of a Mandya wholesale and retail business, from the first summary card to the audit tests. The most useful findings are the numbers that look wrong.

The business story

H Mart is headquartered in Mandya, Karnataka. It sells wholesale across the district with a ten-vehicle fleet and runs one retail showroom in Mandya city. It carries 180 products from 12 brands across six departments, from rice to baby food, and serves 340 customers.

Its owner has four questions. Are we growing profitably? Is stock in the right place? Do we deliver what we promise? Does marketing pay for itself? The report answers them in that order, then turns auditor and tests whether the numbers can be trusted.

The model: three facts, one set of dimensions

The report, page by page

Each page follows one layout: slicers and navigation, a row of cards, two charts, then a table to drill into. Figures in Power BI cards are in millions; the text below uses lakh and crore. Select a screenshot to open it at full size.

Executive Summary

The question

Are we growing profitably?

The answer

Not quite. Net sales rose 6.3%, from ₹8.56 crore to ₹9.10 crore, while contribution fell from ₹79.45 lakh to ₹76.62 lakh. Margin slipped from 9.3% to 8.4%.

The lesson

Put a growth number and a margin number side by side. Either one alone tells a happier story.

Sales Performance

The question

Where do sales come from, and when?

The answer

Wholesale brings 96.6% of sales. The average invoice is ₹23,111. October is the peak in both years, and rice leads the categories at ₹2.42 crore.

The lesson

The YoY card reads 0.0% when no year is selected, because the previous-year calculation then returns the same two-year total. It only means something once a year is chosen.

Profitability

The question

What is left after the cost of serving?

The answer

Gross margin of 11.0% becomes 8.8% contribution. Retail earns 22.8% against wholesale’s 8.3%, so 3.4% of sales brings 8.8% of contribution. Soft drinks are thinnest at 6.7%.

The lesson

A small channel can matter more than its sales suggest. A channel slicer shows it; a total never will.

Inventory

The question

Is the stock right?

The answer

Stock of ₹85.83 lakh covers 22.1 days, or 3.2 weeks. Stockouts run at 0.8%, and 36 products need reordering.

The lesson

Stock is a snapshot, so never add it across weeks. The model keeps it in its own fact table, one row per week, product and location.

Distribution & Service

The question

Do we deliver what we promise?

The answer

On-time is 88.7% and fill rate 93.0%, yet OTIF is only 36.0%. A line counts as OTIF only if it arrives by the promised date and ships the exact quantity ordered.

The lesson

Strict measures expose what averages hide. So does the drill-down: distribution reads 100% in total, but within Baby Food individual brands reach far fewer customers.

Marketing & ROMI

The question

Does marketing pay for itself?

The answer

₹32.61 lakh of spend sits behind ₹3.54 crore of promotion sales, a ROAS of 10.85 times. But those sales earned only ₹12.62 lakh of contribution, so ROMI is −61.3%. Only Dasara Deepavali Mega, at +195%, returned more than it cost.

The lesson

ROAS counts sales and ROMI counts margin. On thin-margin FMCG they can point in opposite directions.

Sales Team & Logistics

The question

Is the team hitting its targets?

The answer

No: 38.6% of target over the two years, 37.4% in FY 2024-25 and 39.8% in FY 2025-26. An early version of this card said 926.3%, because with no date selected it compared 24 months of sales with one month’s target.

The lesson

Test every measure at the total and with no filters. The active count had a similar flaw: it included the “No Salesperson - Showroom” row, so it read 15 instead of 14 and sales per salesperson was wrong.

Now the auditor’s turn

Four pages use the BeIndian.Audit DAX library to ask a different kind of question: does this data behave as it should?

Audit: Descriptive Analytics

The reading

The average line is ₹7,063 and the median ₹2,028, with skewness of 3.77. A few big lines pull the average up, the largest being ₹1,24,038. At least one line lost ₹3,900.

The lesson

Start an audit by looking at the shape. Average alone would mislead you about a typical sale, and the minimum tells you where to look first.

Audit: Top N Percent

The reading

At the 10% setting, the top tenth of sales lines carries ₹9.75 crore, or 55.2% of sales. Chamundi and Cauvery Gold make up the top brands.

The lesson

Audit effort should follow value. Testing a tenth of the lines here covers more than half the rupees.

Audit: Benford’s Law

The reading

First digits of invoice amounts give a MAD of 0.0061, which the report labels acceptable conformity.

The lesson

Conformity is not proof of honesty, and a failure is not proof of fraud. Benford tells you where to look, not what you will find.

Audit: Brand Preference

The reading

Chamundi leads with 1,08,000 units (rounded), 14.2% of quantity, and ₹3.01 crore of sales. Mysuru Select is second by quantity but not among the top three by contribution.

The lesson

Volume and value are different league tables. The word cloud is a custom SVG measure, drawn entirely in DAX.

What to take away

  1. Pair numbers that argue. Sales with margin, ROAS with ROMI, on-time with OTIF.
  2. Model the grain honestly. Sales lines, weekly stock and marketing spend are three facts, not one wide table.
  3. Distrust a number that flatters you. 926% was a filter-context bug that only showed at the total. It took one unfiltered check to catch it.
  4. Then audit it. Shape, concentration, digits and preference take minutes once the functions exist.
